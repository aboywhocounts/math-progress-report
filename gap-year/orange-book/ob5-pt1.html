Chapter 5 (Part 1): Spectra and the Thick Subcategory Theorem

Published

November 17, 2026

In this post we’re finally abandoning spaces and transitioning to focusing on the objects of stable homotopy theory: spectra. I’ll define them and say some things about them, mainly to get myself acquainted with them more since they’re kind of confusing. The other core part of this post is related to the proof of the thick subcategory theorem (TST) for the category \(\FH_{(p)}\), which now denotes something different but I’ll elaborate more later. The proof of the TST will pop out of a reformulation of the nilpotence theorem.

Spectra

As Ravenel notes in the book, the world of spectra is the natural setting for most everything that is discussed. This is simply because the constructions used in the book – homotopy groups, homology, cohomology – are well-behaved with respect to the suspension map. In fact, things get easier especially in the world of homotopy if we allow ourselves to work in the stable range. Take homotopy classes of map \(X\rightarrow Y\) as an example. Working unstably, this set doesn’t have much additional structure that we can work with. When \(X\) is an \(n\)-sphere with \(n\geq 2\), this set becomes an abelian group, and we have some ways to compute them, but most of these groups are unknown. If we instead take stable maps between \(X\) and \(Y\), this set immediately becomes an abelian group (assuming some things about connectivity). Still, we don’t know a lot of these groups, even when \(X\) is a sphere, but they are much easier to work with given this algebraic structure. The stable homotopy category has this additional structure baked in, and the suspension map is inverted, so that we can suspend and desuspend as we wish and things will still make sense.

Definition 1 (Spectrum)

A spectrum \(X\) is a sequence of spaces \(\{X_n\}\) equipped with structure maps \(\sigma_X:\Sigma X_n \rightarrow X_{n+1}\). From any space we may construct its suspension spectrum \(\Sigma^{\infty} X\), with \((\Sigma^{\infty} X)_n=\Sigma^n X\), whose structure maps are just the identity maps. A CW-spectrum is a spectrum that is the suspension spectrum of a CW complex. A CW-spectrum is called finite if the associated CW complex is finite.

The structure maps \(\sigma_X:\Sigma X_n \rightarrow X_{n+1}\) of a spectrum \(X\) are adjoint to maps \(X_n\rightarrow \Omega X_{n+1}\) given that the suspension and loop space functors are adjoint functors. If the latter maps are homotopy equivalences (i.e. dual to the notion of a suspension spectrum), then \(X\) is called an \(\Omega\)-spectrum. This also implies that \(X_n\simeq \Omega^i X_{n+i}\), and \(X\) is called an infinite loop space. These spaces play an important role in homotopy theory. For example the Eilenberg-Maclane spectrum \(HA\) where \(A\) is abelian group is an infinite loop space.

For a spectrum \(X\), we define its homotopy groups by $i(X)={n+i}(X_n). By the Freudenthal's suspension theorem, when $n>i+1$, these are equal, so the limit converges. Another way to interpret this is that for spectra coming from spaces, the homotopy groups of the spectrum are the stable homotopy groups of the associated spaces. For a homology theory $E_*$, we define the $E$-homology of $X$ by $E_i(X)=\varinjlim \overline{E}_{n+i}(X_n). Note that reduced and unreduced homology is used here, which just means we don’t have to distinguish between them with spectra. This makes sense since the suspension map is an isomorphism on reduced homology but not on unreduced homology, so we’re just making sure things behave as they should, similar to how we force ourselves to be working in the stable range with homotopy groups.

We say a spectrum \(X\) is connective if its homotopy groups are bounded below, i.e. if there exists a \(k\) such that \(\pi_i(X)=0\) for \(i<k\). The spectrum \(X\) has finite type if \(\pi_i(X)\) is finitely generated as an abelian group for all \(i\). Another nice thing about working with spectra is that cofibers and fibers agree, up to a dimension shift. If we consider the cofiber sequence

\[ X\xrightarrow{f} Y\rightarrow C_f \]

if we take the homotopy fiber of \(f\), denoted by \(F_f\), then there is a natural map \(g:\Sigma F_f \rightarrow C_f\), and it can be shown that this is a weak equivalence. So for spectra, any cofiber sequence is also a fiber sequence, and either/both may be extended infinitely to the left and right.

Maps of spectra

Now I need to clear up by what is meant by a “map of spectra”, because we need to be precise here. One could naively take a map of spectra \(f:E\rightarrow F\) to just be a sequence of maps \(f_n:E_n\rightarrow F_n\) such that the appropriate diagrams commute. These maps are the morphisms in the category \(\mathbf{Sp}\), but turn out to too restrictive if we want to do computations with general spectra, which is a big part of this book. For finite spectra it’s fine, but I’ll state the general defintion as well. I’ll note that the defintion that follows will actually define homotopy classes of maps between spectra, i.e. the morphisms in the homotopy category \(\mathbf{HoSp}\). This is the category that Ravenel will implicitly be working over, thus whenever I say \(f\) is a map of spectra, I will always mean \(f\) is a homotopy class of maps, unless explicitly stated otherwise.

If the target of a map of spectra $f:E\(F\) is an \(\Omega\)-spectrum, then if \(f_n\simeq \Omega f_{n+1}\) for all \(n\) is the correct definition, but doesn’t work in general. It seems that unless the target is an \(\Omega\)-spectrum, a single \(F_n\) won’t necessarily contain all of the “stable information” of the entire spectrum. More precisely, when \(F_n\) is an \(\Omega\)-spectrum, all of the information in the spectrum is contained in the zero-th space \(F_0\), simply because we know that \(F_n\simeq \Omega^n F_0\). This isn’t the case for a general spectrum, and applying the loop space functor is not an equivalence of objects, resulting in differences between the individual spaces \(F_n\). (This isn’t very rigorous, but it’s my understanding of it and I felt it was important to have some sort of explanation as to why the other definition isn’t sufficient.) To remedy this deficiency, we replace the spectrum \(F\) with a homotopy equivalent \(\Omega\)-spectrum \(\widetilde{F}\) as follows: let \(\widetilde{F}_n\) be defined by

\[ \widetilde{F_n}= \varinjlim \Omega^k F_{n+k}\left( = \varinjlim (\Omega F_n \xrightarrow{\Omega} \Omega^2 F_n \xrightarrow{\Omega} \Omega^3 F_n \rightarrow \cdots) \right) \]

then \(\widetilde{F}_n\simeq \Omega \widetilde{F}_{n+1}\) since we’re taking the colimit, so that \(\widetilde{F}\) is an \(\Omega\)-spectrum. Now, we need to show that \(\pi_n(F)\cong \pi_n(\widetilde{F})\) for all \(n\). Ultimately, this pops right out of the definitons and using the fact that filtered colimits commute with the homotopy groups functor (spheres are compact).

We thus have the following definition:

Definition 2 (Map of spectra (in \(\mathbf{HoSp}\)))

A map of spectra \(f:E\rightarrow F\) is a collection of maps of the form \[ E_n\xrightarrow{f_n} \widetilde{F_n}= \varinjlim \Omega^kF_{n+k} \]

such that \(f_n\simeq \Omega f_{n+1}\).

If \(E\) and \(F\) are suspension spectra, then the collection of maps \(\{f_n\}\) can be obtained by \(f_n\) for a fixed \(n\) using the adjunction of the suspension and loop space functors. With this defintion, for a spectrum \(E\), we define the \(E\)-cohomology of a spectrum \(X\) to be

\[ E^k(X)=[X,\Sigma^k E]=[\Sigma^{-k} X, E]=[X,E]_{-k} \]

Smash products

There is a natural way to take products of spectra called the smash product. For spaces \(X\) and \(Y\), the smash product \(X \wedge Y\) is defined to be the quotient space \(X\times Y/(X\times \{y_0\} \cup \{x_0\} \times Y)\). For spectra, the definition is actually quite complicated, so I won’t go into too much detail here. For the purposes of the book, we can take the smash product of two spectra \(E\wedge F\) to be given by

\[ (E\wedge F)_{2n}=E_n \wedge F_n \qquad \qquad (E\wedge F)_{2n+1}=\Sigma E_n\wedge F_n \]

with structure maps

\[ \Sigma E_n \wedge \Sigma F_n =\Sigma (E\wedge F)_{2n+1} \xrightarrow{\sigma_E \wedge \sigma_F} (E\wedge F)_{2n+2}= E_{n+1} \wedge F_{n+1} \] \[ \Sigma E_n \wedge F_n = S^1\wedge E_n\wedge F_n= \Sigma(E_n\wedge F_n)=\Sigma (E\wedge F)_{2n} \rightarrow (E\wedge F)_{2n+1}= \Sigma E_n\wedge F_n \]

the last composition comes from the appropriate identity map and that the smash product is associative, which can be shown. It can also be shown to be commutative.

Brave new algebra

The smash product is analogous to the tensor product in the category of abelian groups \(\mathbf{Ab}\). This category has a symmetric monoidal structure given by the triple \((\mathbf{Ab},\otimes,\ZZ)\), which just means the category forms a monoid with respect to the tensor product and the multiplicative unit is the integers \(\ZZ\). For spectra, both \(\mathbf{HoSp}\) and \(\mathbf{Sp}\) are equipped with symmetric monoidal structure given by \((\mathbf{Sp},\wedge, S^0)\), so that the monoid operation is the smash product and the unit is the sphere spectrum \(S^0\). I have to note that there are some intricacies to the symmetric monoidal structure of \(\Sp\) that I won’t go into here. The right notion of the smash product itself is hard to formulate for plain spectra, and work must be done to make it behave as we wish (i.e. for it to be associative and commutative like \(\otimes\)). There is a way to make everything nice, but it’s beyond me and the scope of what I’ll be writing.

Just as we can use the symmetric monoidal structure of \(\Ab\) to contruct commutative algebraic objects such as rings and modules and do interesting things with them, we can construct analogous objects in \(\HoSp\). Precisely, if we have a spectrum \(E\) together with a multiplication map \(E \wedge E \rightarrow E\) and unit map \(\eta:S^0\rightarrow E\) such that a few diagrams are commutative (up to homotopy). A module spectrum \(M\) over \(E\) is one equipped with a map \(E \wedge M \rightarrow M\), where some other diagrams commute (also up to homotopy). We can actually extend this construction to \(\mathbf{Sp}\), with some more work, and this actually makes things very interesting. This is because maps between spectra in this category have a much richer structure: while \(\mathbf{HoSp}\) is enriched over \(\mathbf{Ab}\), \(\mathbf{Sp}\) is instead enriched over \(\mathbf{Top}\)––the category of topological spaces, which has a much richer structure. This allows us to talk about homotopies between maps of spectra, and homotopies between homotopies, and so on. The complex structure of the mapping spaces makes the theory of spectra very interesting and deeply intertwined with algebra. Indeed, this discussion sets the stage for doing more complex algebra (and even algebraic geometry) with spectra. This area of research was dubbed “brave new algebra”, which is pretty apt name if you ask me. If you’re interested in this sort of perspective I urge you to check out this lecture given by Tomer Schlank here. For me it was really eye-opening overview of the “brave new algebra” philosophy and kickstarted my interest in stable homotopy theory.

Let’s return to the smash product. For a spectrum \(X\) and a map of spectra \(f:X\rightarrow Y\), we define \(X^{(k)}=\bigwedge^k X\) to be the \(k\)-fold smash product and \(f^{(k)}:X^{(k)}\rightarrow Y^{(k)}\) is the evident map.

Definition 3 A map \(f:X\rightarrow Y\) is smash nilpotent if \(f^{(k)}\) is nullhomotopic for some \(k\).

For a homology theory \(E_*\), another equivalent definition of the \(E\)-homology of a spectrum \(X\) is \(E_*(X)=\pi_*(E\wedge X)\). Using this, we can rewrite the nilpotence theorem as follows:

Theorem 1 (Nilpotence theorem (smash product form))

Let \(f:F\rightarrow X\) be a map of spectra where \(F\) is finite. Then \(f\) is smash nilpotent if the map \(MU\wedge f:MU\wedge F \rightarrow MU\wedge X\) is nullhomotopic.

This is clearly rewriting the nilpotence theorem in a more natural way for spectra: \(MU\) detects nilpotent maps (i.e. homotopically trivial maps) in \(\mathbf{HoSp}\) just as it did with spaces.

An important corollary is the following:

Theorem 2 Let \(W\),\(X\), and \(Y\) all be \(p\)-local finite spectra and let \(f:X\rightarrow Y\) be a map. Then the map \(W\wedge f^{(k)}\) is nullhomotopic for \(k\) sufficiently large if the induced map \(K(n)_*(W\wedge f)\) is the trivial homomorphism for all \(n\geq 0\).

It is from this corollary that we will prove the TST. This post is getting a little long, so I’ll break it into two. This concludes the first part.
