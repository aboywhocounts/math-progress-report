Chapter 4: Morava things

Published

November 10, 2026

In this post I’ll be discussing ideas due to Jack Morava. Eventually, we’ll need to do computations in \(MU\)-theory, and the group \(\Gamma\) is important due to the earlier identification of its relation to multiplicative cohomology operations in \(MU\)-theory. The action of \(\Gamma\) on the Lazard ring \(L\) will give us a new perspective on how to study this group and will naturally motivate us to understand an analogous group of multiplicative operations in Morava K-theory.

First, let’s examine the action of \(\Gamma\) on some objects geometrically. Let \(H_{\ZZ}L\) denote the set of ring homomorphisms \(\theta:L \rightarrow \ZZ\). This set inherits a \(\Gamma\)-action due to the known action of \(\Gamma\) on \(L\). \(L\) is a polynomial ring, so \(\theta\) is determined by where it sends the polynomial generators \(x_i\), which makes \(H_{\ZZ}L\) into an infinite-dimensional affine space over \(\ZZ\). Now, from the earlier discussion on FGLs, points in \(H_{\ZZ}L\) simply correspond to FGLs over \(\ZZ\). The group \(\Gamma\) will act on a FGL (over \(\ZZ\)) \(F(x,y)\) corresponding to \(\theta\) by \(\gamma^{-1}(F(\gamma(x),\gamma(y)))\), coming from how we defined the action of \(\Gamma\) on \(L\). Now, if we have two FGLs \(F(x,y)\) and \(G(x,y)\) that are in the same \(\Gamma\)-orbit (i.e. \(F(x,y)=\gamma *G(x,y)=\gamma^{-1}G(\gamma(x),\gamma(y))\) for some \(\gamma\in\Gamma\)), then this also implies that

\[ \gamma(F(x,y))=G(\gamma(x),\gamma(y)) \]

which is what we call an isomorphism of FGLs. This implication goes both ways so \(\Gamma\)-orbits directly correspond to isomorphism classes of FGLs. As for the stabilizer groups, these will correspond to so called strict automorphism groups of a FGL represented by \(\theta\). Additionally, if \(\theta\) and \(\theta'\) are in the same orbit, then their strict automorphism groups will be conjugate subgroups of \(\Gamma\). A strict automorphism of a FGL (over \(\ZZ\)) \(F(x,y)\) is a power series (over \(\ZZ\)) that is a self-isomorphism of \(F\) and has the from \(x + \text{higher terms}\).

The theory of FGLs over \(\ZZ\) is complicated so let’s change the base ring to the field \(k\), the algebraic closure of \(\FF_p\). Our space of interest is \(H_k L\), which is an infinite dimensional vector space over \(k\), analogous to the \(\ZZ\) case. Translating the above results over and using the classification theorem, we see that there is exactly one \(\Gamma_k\)-orbit for each height \(n\). Additionally, up to conjugacy, we have a unique strict automorphism group of a height \(n\) FGL. We denote this group by \(S_n\), and the corresponding FGL of interest will be denoted by \(F_n\). The notation \(\Gamma_k\) is used because now that we have changed rings, we need to make the appropriate changes for the base rings of isomorphisms and automorphisms of FGLs; they both need to now be power series over \(k\) with extra conditions. So, really we have the group of power series over \(k\) of the form

\[ x+b_1 x^2 +b_2 x^3+\cdots \]

where \(b_i\in k\) and the group operation is composition . This is exactly \(\Gamma\) with a different coefficient ring.

Using the action of \(\Gamma\), we can decompose \(H_k L\) into simpler constituents, similar to how we broke down the categories \(\CG_{(p)}\) and \(\FH_{(p)}\) in the previous chapter. For \(\theta\in H_k L\), this corresponds to an FGL over \(k\). We know that \(\theta(v_i)\) will be the \(k\)-coefficient of \(x^{p^i}\) in the \(p\)-series \([p](x)\) of \(\theta\). If \(\theta\) has height \(n\), then by definition, \(\theta(v_i)=0\) for \(i<n\) and \(\theta(v_n)\neq 0\).

Letting \(Y_i \coloneqq \{\theta\in H_k L: \theta(v_j)=0\; \text{for}\; j<i \;\& \;\theta(v_i)\neq 0\}\), this is precisely the height \(n\) orbit. If we take \(X_n\coloneqq \bigcup_{n\leq i} Y_i\), this yields a nested sequence of subsets

\[ H_k L=X_1\supset X_2\supset X_3\supset X_4\cdots \]

The group \(S_n\)

We now turn our attention to the group \(S_n\), the strict automorphism group of the height \(n\) FGL over \(k\). In what follows, we’ll describe \(S_n\) and how it can be constructed for the sake of completeness. The description is quite involved and involves a hefty amount of algebraic number theory, which I find confusing because I’m not very familiar with the area.

First, recall that the field \(\FF_{p^n}\) is degree \(n\) extension of \(\FF_p\) obtained by adjoining a primitive \((p^n-1)^{\text{st}}\) root of unity \(\overline{\zeta}\), which is the root of some irreducible degree \(n\) polynomial. Its Galois group is \(C_n\), the cyclic group of order \(n\) generated by the Frobenius map. There is an analogous construction for \(\ZZ_p\), the \(p\)-adic integers, yielding a degree \(n\) extension denoted \(W(\FF_{p^n})\). The Frobenius map has a lift \({\sigma}\) that fixes \(\ZZ_p\) and the root of the appropriate polynomial \(\zeta\) is congruent \(mod p\) to \(\overline{\zeta}\). Additionally \(\sigma(\zeta)=\zeta^p\) and \(\sigma(x)=x^p\mod p\) for \(x\in W(\FF_{p^n})\)

The fraction field of \(W(\FF_{p^n})\) is denoted by \(K_n\), which is some special degree \(n\) extension of \(\QQ_p\). We then adjoin a noncommuting power series variable \(S\) satisfuing \(Sx=\sigma(x)S\) for \(x\in K_n\). For reasons I won’t explain, \(S\) commutes with all of \(\QQ_p\) and \(S^n\) commutes with all of \(K_n\), which allows us to form the division algebra

\[ D_n \coloneqq K_n\langle S\rangle/(S^n-p) \]

This division algebra contains a maximal order (which is kind of like a ring of integers but more complicated I believe)

\[ E_n \coloneqq W(\FF_{p^n})\langle S\rangle/(S^n-p) \]

with some nice properties. We may write elements uniquely as

\[ a=\sum_{0\leq i\leq n-1} a_i S^i \]

with \(a_i \in W(\FF_{p^n})\), or

\[ a=\sum_{i\geq 0} e_i S^i \]

where \(e_i\) satisfies \(e_i^{p^n}-e_i=0\), so that \(e_i\) is either 0 or a root of unity. The group of units \(E^{\times}_n\subset E_n\) is the subgroup with \(e_0\neq 0\) or equivalently with \(a_0\) a unit in \(W(\FF_{p^n})\). It can be shown that the full automorphism group of a height \(n\) FGL over \(k\) is isomorphic to \(E^{\times}_n\) and the strict automorphism group \(S_n\) is isomorphic to the subgroup of \(E^{\times}_n\) with \(e_0=1\).

So we have a description of what \(S_n\) looks like. Since there is only one orbit for each height \(n\), we really only need to look at one FGL for each height, which we denoted by \(F_n\). To define it, we first need the notion of a logarithm of a FGL.

Definition 1 Let \(F(x,y)\) be a formal group law over a torsion-free ring \(R\). Let \(F_2(x,y)=\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}\). We define the logarithm of \(F\) by the formula

\[ \log_F(x)=\int_0^x \frac{dt}{F_2(t,0)} \]

Some quick examples: 1. For the additive FGL \(F(x,y)=x+y\), \(F_2(x,y)=1\) and \(\log_F(x)=x\) 2. For the multiplicative FGL \(F(x,y)=x+y+xy\), \(F_2(x,y)=1+x\) and so \(\log_F(x)=\int_0^x \frac{dt}{1+t}=\log(1+x)\)

Resuming our discussion, let \(F\) denote the FGL over \(\ZZp\) satisfying

\[ \log_F(x)=\sum_{i\geq 0} \frac{x^{p^{in}}}{p^i} \]

FGLs over rings of characteristic zero are uniquely determined by their logarithms, so defining \(F\) by its logarithm is no problem. We then define \(F_n\) to be the FGL obtained from \(F\) by reducing it mod \(p\) and tensoring with \(\FF_{p^n}\).
