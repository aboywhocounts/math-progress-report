Chapter 4: Results due to Jack Morava

Published

November 12, 2026

In this post I’ll be discussing ideas due to Jack Morava. Eventually, we’ll need to do computations in \(MU\)-theory, and the group \(\Gamma\) is important due to the earlier identification of its relation to multiplicative cohomology operations in \(MU\)-theory. The action of \(\Gamma\) on the Lazard ring \(L\) will give us a new perspective on how to study this group and will naturally motivate us to understand an analogous group of multiplicative operations in Morava K-theory.

Orbits and stabilizers of the \(\Gamma\)-action

First, let’s examine the action of \(\Gamma\) on some objects geometrically. Let \(H_{\ZZ}L\) denote the set of ring homomorphisms \(\theta:L \rightarrow \ZZ\). This set inherits a \(\Gamma\)-action due to the known action of \(\Gamma\) on \(L\). \(L\) is a polynomial ring, so \(\theta\) is determined by where it sends the polynomial generators \(x_i\), which makes \(H_{\ZZ}L\) into an infinite-dimensional affine space over \(\ZZ\). Now, from the earlier discussion on FGLs, points in \(H_{\ZZ}L\) simply correspond to FGLs over \(\ZZ\). The group \(\Gamma\) will act on a FGL (over \(\ZZ\)) \(F(x,y)\) corresponding to \(\theta\) by \(\gamma^{-1}(F(\gamma(x),\gamma(y)))\), coming from how we defined the action of \(\Gamma\) on \(L\). Now, if we have two FGLs \(F(x,y)\) and \(G(x,y)\) that are in the same \(\Gamma\)-orbit (i.e. \(F(x,y)=\gamma *G(x,y)=\gamma^{-1}G(\gamma(x),\gamma(y))\) for some \(\gamma\in\Gamma\)), then this also implies that

\[ \gamma(F(x,y))=G(\gamma(x),\gamma(y)) \]

which is what we call an isomorphism of FGLs. This implication goes both ways so \(\Gamma\)-orbits directly correspond to isomorphism classes of FGLs. As for the stabilizer groups, these will correspond to so called strict automorphism groups of a FGL represented by \(\theta\). Additionally, if \(\theta\) and \(\theta'\) are in the same orbit, then their strict automorphism groups will be conjugate subgroups of \(\Gamma\). A strict automorphism of a FGL (over \(\ZZ\)) \(F(x,y)\) is a power series (over \(\ZZ\)) that is a self-isomorphism of \(F\) and has the from \(x + \text{higher terms}\).

The theory of FGLs over \(\ZZ\) is complicated so let’s change the base ring to the field \(k\), the algebraic closure of \(\FF_p\). Our space of interest is \(H_k L\), which is an infinite dimensional vector space over \(k\), analogous to the \(\ZZ\) case. Translating the above results over and using the classification theorem, we see that there is exactly one \(\Gamma_k\)-orbit for each height \(n\). Additionally, up to conjugacy, we have a unique strict automorphism group of a height \(n\) FGL. We denote this group by \(S_n\), and the corresponding FGL of interest will be denoted by \(F_n\) (also called the Honda FGL). Crucially, while this clean and rigid orbit structure only exists when working over \(k\), the Honda FGL is actually defined over the finite field \(\FF_{p^n}\), not its algebraic closure \(k\) (same algebraic closure as \(\FF_p\)). We will elaborate more on this apparent coefficient mismatch later.

The notation \(\Gamma_k\) is used because now that we have changed rings, we need to make the appropriate changes for the base rings of isomorphisms and automorphisms of FGLs; they both need to now be power series over \(k\) with extra conditions. So, really we have the group of power series over \(k\) of the form

\[ x+b_1 x^2 +b_2 x^3+\cdots \]

where \(b_i\in k\) and the group operation is composition . This is exactly \(\Gamma\) with a different coefficient ring.

Using the action of \(\Gamma\), we can decompose \(H_k L\) into simpler constituents, similar to how we broke down the categories \(\mathbf{C \Gamma}_{(p)}\) and \(\FH_{(p)}\) in the previous chapter. For \(\theta\in H_k L\), this corresponds to an FGL over \(k\). We know that \(\theta(v_i)\) will be the \(k\)-coefficient of \(x^{p^i}\) in the \(p\)-series \([p](x)\) of \(\theta\). If \(\theta\) has height \(n\), then by definition, \(\theta(v_i)=0\) for \(i<n\) and \(\theta(v_n)\neq 0\).

Letting \(Y_i = \{\theta\in H_k L: \theta(v_j)=0\; \text{for}\; j<i \;\& \;\theta(v_i)\neq 0\}\), this is precisely the height \(n\) orbit. If we take \(X_n= \bigcup_{n\leq i} Y_i\), this yields a nested sequence of subsets

\[ H_k L=X_1\supset X_2\supset X_3\supset X_4\cdots \]

The group \(S_n\)

We now turn our attention to the group \(S_n\), the strict automorphism group of the FGL over \(\FF_{p^n}\) of height \(n\), \(F_n\). I’ll go through a description of \(S_n\) that is independent of \(F_n\), and then I’ll say something about how they are related. The group \(S_n\) can be defined purely through algebraic number theory, and is an interesting object to study in its own right.

First, recall that the field \(\FF_{p^n}\) is degree \(n\) extension of \(\FF_p\) obtained by adjoining a primitive \((p^n-1)^{\text{st}}\) root of unity \(\overline{\zeta}\), which is the root of some irreducible degree \(n\) polynomial. Its Galois group is \(C_n\), the cyclic group of order \(n\) generated by the Frobenius map. There is an analogous construction for \(\ZZ_p\), the \(p\)-adic integers, yielding a degree \(n\) extension denoted \(W(\FF_{p^n})\). The Frobenius map has a lift \({\sigma}\) that fixes \(\ZZ_p\) and the root of the appropriate polynomial \(\zeta\) is congruent \(mod p\) to \(\overline{\zeta}\). Additionally \(\sigma(\zeta)=\zeta^p\) and \(\sigma(x)=x^p\mod p\) for \(x\in W(\FF_{p^n})\)

The fraction field of \(W(\FF_{p^n})\) is denoted by \(K_n\), which is some special degree \(n\) extension of \(\QQ_p\). We then adjoin a noncommuting power series variable \(S\) satisfying \(Sx=\sigma(x)S\) for \(x\in K_n\). For reasons I won’t explain, \(S\) commutes with all of \(\QQ_p\) and \(S^n\) commutes with all of \(K_n\), which allows us to form the division algebra

\[ D_n = K_n\langle S\rangle/(S^n-p) \]

This division algebra contains a maximal order (which is kind of like a ring of integers but more complicated I believe)

\[ E_n = W(\FF_{p^n})\langle S\rangle/(S^n-p) \]

with some nice properties. We may write elements uniquely as

\[ a=\sum_{0\leq i\leq n-1} a_i S^i \]

with \(a_i \in W(\FF_{p^n})\), or

\[ a=\sum_{i\geq 0} e_i S^i \]

where each \(e_i\in W(\FF_{p^n})\) satisfies \(e_i^{p^n}-e_i=0\), so that \(e_i\) is either 0 or a root of unity. The group of units \(E^{\times}_n\subset E_n\) is the subgroup with \(e_0\neq 0\) or equivalently with \(a_0\) a unit in \(W(\FF_{p^n})\). It can be shown that the full automorphism group of a height \(n\) FGL over \(k\) is isomorphic to \(E^{\times}_n\) and the strict automorphism group \(S_n\) is isomorphic to the subgroup of \(E^{\times}_n\) with \(e_0=1\).

Let’s move on to the Honda formal group law. Its definition involves the notion of a logarithm of a FGL which is defined as follows:

Definition 1 (Logarithm of a formal group law)

Let \(F(x,y)\) be a formal group law over a torsion-free ring \(R\). Let \(F_2(x,y)=\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}\). We define the logarithm of \(F\) by the formula

\[ \log_F(x)=\int_0^x \frac{dt}{F_2(t,0)} \]

Some quick examples:

  1. For the additive FGL \(F(x,y)=x+y\), \(F_2(x,y)=1\) and \(\log_F(x)=x\)

  2. For the multiplicative FGL \(F(x,y)=x+y+xy\), \(F_2(x,y)=1+x\) and so \(\log_F(x)=\int_0^x \frac{dt}{1+t}=\log(1+x)\)

Resuming our discussion, let \(F\) denote the FGL over \(\ZZp\) satisfying

\[ \log_F(x)=\sum_{i\geq 0} \frac{x^{p^{in}}}{p^i} \]

FGLs over rings of characteristic zero are uniquely determined by their logarithms, so defining \(F\) by its logarithm is no problem. We then define \(F_n\) to be the FGL obtained from \(F\) by reducing it mod \(p\) and tensoring with \(\FF_{p^n}\). Tensoring with \(\FF_{p^n}\) is what forces \(F_n\) to reside over this field. As it stands, and as mentioned before, there seems to be a bit of a coefficient mismatch between \(S_n\) and \(F_n\). The former group is coming from a division algebra coming from an extension of the \(p\)-adic numbers, a field of characteristic zero, where the other has roots (no pun intended) in \(\FF_{p^n}\). On the other hand, these two objects are very intimately connected. In fact, as shown in Ravenel’s Green Book (Appendix 2.2), the endomorphism ring of the Honda FGL \(F_n\) is isomorphic to the maximal order \(E_n\) that we saw earlier, which implies that the strict automorphism group is indeed \(S_n\). Examining the definitions, this means that we would need to find a way to write an arbitrary element \(a\) of \(S_n\) as power series with coefficients in \(\FF_{p^n}\). We know we can uniquely write \(a=\sum_{i\geq 0} e_i S^i\) subject to the condition that \(e_0=1\). The \(e_i\) live in a characteristic zero ring \(W(\FF_{p^n})\), but are subject to the relations \(e_i^{p^n}-e_i=0\). These \(e_i\) are known as Teichmüller representatives. They are special because for any \(\alpha\in \FF_{p^n}\), there is a unique \(\overline{\alpha}\in W(\FF_{p^n})\) such that \(\overline{\alpha}^{p^n}-\overline{\alpha}=0\) and \(\alpha=\overline{\alpha} \mod p\). Thus we can safely regard the \(e_i\) as living in \(\FF_{p^n}\), which fixes our problem. This isn’t made explicit in Ravenel’s book but is important to understand all that’s happening with the coefficients. It was very unclear to me how this all worked out, and is touched on in much more detail in Appendix 2.2 of the Green Book.

To summarize, \(S_n\) acts on \(F_n\) by automorphisms \(e\) which are power series such that \(e(F_n(x,y))=F_n(e(x),e(y))\). These power series have the form

\[ e(x)=\sum^{F_n}_{i\geq 0} e_i x^{p^i} \]

where we have made the identification with \(e_i\) and elements of \(\FF_{p^n}\) implictly. The superscript \(F_n\) means that the arithmetic is dictated by the FGL \(F_n\), not regular addition. Explicitly, instead of \(x+e_1x^p+e_2x^{p^2}\), the first few terms are actually

\[ F_n(e^2x^{p^2},F_n(x,e_1x^{p})) \]

Cohomology of \(S_n\)

The last section of this chapter is devoted to studying the cohomology of \(S_n\) and some of its group-theoretic properties. The layout in the book is more-or-less a presentation of a bunch of results, some of which I’ll state here and some I won’t. I’ll also provide some justification as to why the cohomology of \(S_n\) is relevant, but also why \(S_n\) is itself, apart from what was discussed above.

First we’ll tackle the importance of \(S_n\). In an earlier post, I discussed how \(\Gamma\) can essentially be identified with the group of multiplicative cohomology operations of \(MU\)-theory. \(S_n\) plays an analogous role in Morava K-theory, but the crucial part is that the algebra of all cohomology operations in Morava K-theory can be recovered from just these multiplicative operations, which is not the case for other theories. For example, in ordinary mod 2 cohomology, the algebra of stable operations is the Steenrod algebra \(\mathcal{A}\), but the only stable multiplicative cohomology operations are the zero operation and the identity. This is due to the Cartan formula, and the only multiplicative operation that can be constructed from stable ones is the total square \(\Sq=\sum_{i\geq 0} \Sq^i\), but this isn’t actually in \(\mathcal{A}\) since it’s an infinite sum. The same is true for mod \(p\) cohomology and \(MU\)-theory. For Morava K-theory there are a few other necessary details to extend the information about \(S_n\) to the full algebra, but it’s doable.

As for its cohomology, computations of homotopy groups in classical homotopy theory involved the Adams spectral sequence, which involved a lot of computations of Ext over the Steenrod algebra \(\mathcal{A}\). These Ext groups can be interpreted as the cohomology of the Steenrod algebra. Eventually we’ll need to compute \(K(n)_*(X)\) for spaces/spectra, and to do this we’ll need information about the cohomology of \(S_n\) with various coefficients.

Clearly, \(S_n\) is quite complicated, Ravenel writes that it’s profinite and is describable as a “\(p\)-adic Lie group”. Therefore, the nuances of the calculations won’t be touched on. The cohomological calculations are made using “continuous mod \(p\) cohomology”, but I confess I don’t really understand what that means. The full picture is described in Chapter 6 of the Green Book.

Theorem 1  

  1. \(H^*(S_n)\) is finitely generated as an algebra.

  2. If \(n\) is not divisible by \(p-1\), then \(H^i(S_n) = 0\) for \(i > n^2\), and for \(0 \leq i \leq n^2\), \[H^i(S_n) = H^{n^2-i}(S_n),\]

    i.e., \(H^*(S_n)\) has cohomological dimension \(n^2\) and satisfies Poincaré duality.

  3. If \(p-1\) does divide \(n\), then \(H^*(S_n)\) is periodic, i.e., there is an \(x \in H^{2i}(S_n)\) for some \(i > 0\) such that \(H^*(S_n)\) is a finitely generated free module over \(\mathbf{Z}/(p)[x]\).

Part 1 helps us with the computational complexity of the cohomology of \(S_n\), and part 2 tells us that when \(S_n\) is torsion-free (\(S_n\) has no elements of finite order prime to \(p\), since it’s a pro-\(p\)-group, i.e. a projective limit of finite \(p\)-groups), its cohomology groups have nice symmetry and there is a nice bound on how complicated they get.

Theorem 2 All finite abelian subgroups of \(S_n\) are cyclic. \(S_n\) contains an element of order \(p^{i+1}\) if and only if \(n\) is divisible by \((p-1)p^i\).

The second part of this theorem underlies the cohomological results of the previous one. I’m sure there is much more to be said about these results, but conclusions besides the ones I’ve already stated are beyond me. They are clearly very important when computations need to be made, so I’ll probably revisit them at a later point.

This concludes this post. We’ve seen a much more developed algebraic picture regarding the action of \(\Gamma\) and the Morava stabilizer groups \(S_n\). This chapter was quite difficult to follow due to the hefty amount of algebra and number theory involved, but I enjoyed reading it and working through it.
